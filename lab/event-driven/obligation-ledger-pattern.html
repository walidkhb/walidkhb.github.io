The Obligation Ledger Pattern: Modelling Court and Regulatory Holds Without Stacking Core Holds

Every bank receives legal orders: block an amount, garnish a balance, deny a service, ban a customer from an activity, lift what an earlier order imposed. The obvious design places one core hold per order and lets the core banking system be the record. It works for the first order and breaks on the third against the same account. This guide covers the alternative: record obligations, keep the money in core, and treat the core hold as an idempotent, reconciled projection.

Overview

Judicial enforcement looks simple from a distance. A court or a competent authority sends an order. The bank locates the subject’s assets and acts. There are five verbs. Block reserves an amount. Garnish reserves an amount, or freezes the asset when no amount is given. Deny and ban restrict what the subject can do, not how much is held. Lift releases what an earlier order imposed, in full or in part.

The difficulty is never a single verb. It is the combination. Several orders from different issuers can target the same account at the same time. Each can be lifted, reduced or reversed independently. A reversal can arrive while the original order is still executing. And the bank must be able to answer, at any moment and for any auditor: what is held, on which asset, because of which order, of which type, and how much was left untouched by law?

That question has one architectural fork. Either the core banking system is the record of what is held, or the enforcement platform is. This guide takes the second path and walks through what it costs: a ledger, a reconciler, an event store, a service-definition model and a connector contract. It builds on Event Sourcing & CQRS in Payments and does not repeat those basics. The focus here is on what changes when the aggregate is a legal obligation rather than a payment.

Figure 1 — Orders write to the obligation ledger; the reconciler projects one hold per asset into core. Money stays in core. The ledger is the record of why it is held.

Why stacking one hold per order fails

The default design delegates everything to core. Each order places its own hold, tagged with the order reference. A lift removes that order’s holds. Core is the record and the integration layer is stateless. It is the simplest design, it passes the first round of testing, and it fails in ways that only appear in production:

  • Holds are sized blind. Three orders on one account produce three holds, each sized without knowledge of the others. When the balance cannot cover all three, which one is short is decided by arrival order, not by law or policy.
  • Partial lifts need surgery. A partial lift must find the right hold and resize it. Many core hold primitives support only place and remove, so a partial lift becomes “remove and re-place”, with a window in which the money is free.
  • Some assets cannot carry a reference. Cards and wallets often expose a hold primitive with no reference field at all. Once two orders touch a prepaid card, nobody can tell which hold belongs to which.
  • Totals are reconstructed by scanning. “How much is held across all orders for this customer?” requires walking every core system and stitching records back to orders by hand.
  • Reversal is choreography. Undoing an order mid-execution means knowing which holds exist at this instant and which calls are in flight, then compensating each one.
ConcernStacked core holdsObligation ledger
Accumulation across ordersImplicit; first-come, first-heldExplicit sum per asset, by type
Partial liftFind and resize a specific holdAppend a release; target recomputes
Crude core primitivesLeaks into every serviceAbsorbed by the reconciler and connector
“Why is this held?”Manual stitchingOne query against the ledger
Cards and walletsSecond-class, often unreferenceableSame obligation model as accounts
Operational simplicityHigh: no extra stateLower: drift must be detected and healed

The ledger loses on exactly one row, and it is worth being honest about it. It introduces a reconciliation obligation: drift between the ledger and core must be detected and healed. That cost is bounded and testable. The correctness cost of stacking is neither.

The split that makes this acceptable

The platform owns obligations. Core owns money. The ledger never holds a balance, never posts an entry and never moves funds. That keeps the enforcement platform out of the money-ledger business and leaves the regulator’s view of balances exactly where it was. What changes is that “why is this held?” has one authoritative answer.

Record obligations, not money

An obligation is a typed claim that an order places on one asset. Types matter because they behave differently. An amount hold accumulates. A restriction does not. A protected allowance subtracts. Types also let different measures coexist on one account and be lifted independently: lifting a ban must not release a garnish.

Obligation typeProduced byAccumulates?Core projection
AMOUNT_HOLDBlock, garnish with an amountYes — summed per assetOne hold sized by the reconciler
FREEZEGarnish without an amountNo — boolean per assetFreeze flag or full hold, per connector capability
RESTRICTIONDeny, banNo — set of kinds per party or assetApplied once, removed when the set is empty
PROTECTED_ALLOWANCEPolicy, per pay cycleRenews once per cycleReduces the target; never projected on its own

Lift is not an obligation type. A lift is an event that releases, fully or partly, the obligations an earlier order created. The ledger records the release and the reconciler does the rest. This is the central simplification: nobody ever writes code that says “find hold X in core and reduce it by Y”.

Obligations are allocated per asset. When an order targets a party rather than an account, an allocation policy decides which assets carry how much: transactional accounts first, a currency preference, eligibility rules per asset class. Allocation is policy, so it lives in versioned configuration, not in connector code.

Every row carries sourceEvent. The ledger is itself a projection of the order streams described below: you can drop it and rebuild it. That property is what lets you trust it as the record.

The core hold as a reconciled projection

The reconciler has one job: for each asset, compute the hold that should exist and make core agree. The target is one line:

targettext
heldTarget = min( netObligation, max(0, balance − protectedAllowance) )

netObligation is the sum of active amount holds on the asset, minus releases. protectedAllowance is the part of income the law leaves with the customer, or zero when core protects it natively or the order waives it. balance is the balance before the platform’s own hold. Get that last detail wrong and the reconciler chases its own tail: it sees the hold it placed as missing money and shrinks the hold on every run.

The reconciler runs on four triggers: an obligation changes; a balance-changing event lands on the asset, such as a credit that makes more money available to hold; a pay cycle rolls over and the allowance renews; a periodic sweep checks for drift. All four call the same function. One path means one thing to test.

HoldReconciler.javajava
public final class HoldReconciler {

    private final ObligationLedger ledger;
    private final ConnectorRuntime connectors;
    private final ProjectionStore projections;   // last hold placed by the platform, per asset
    private final DriftReporter drift;

    public Outcome reconcile(AssetRef asset, Trigger trigger) {
        LedgerView view = ledger.activeView(asset);           // all orders, all types
        Money net        = view.netAmountObligation();
        Money protectedAl = view.protectedAllowance();         // ZERO if delegated or waived

        HoldState core   = connectors.holdState(asset);          // what core says exists
        Money balance    = core.availableExcludingOwnHold();    // never count the platform's own hold

        Money target = Money.min(net,
                Money.max(Money.ZERO, balance.minus(protectedAl)));

        Projection expected = projections.get(asset);
        if (!core.heldAmount().equals(expected.amount())) {
            drift.report(asset, expected, core, trigger);      // someone else touched it
        }
        if (core.heldAmount().equals(target)) {
            return Outcome.noOp(asset, view.version());       // idempotent: nothing to do
        }

        // Key = asset + ledger version. A replay of the same version is a no-op
        // at the connector; a newer version supersedes, never stacks.
        IdempotencyKey key = IdempotencyKey.of(asset, view.version());
        CapabilityResult r = target.isZero()
                ? connectors.holdLift(asset, key)
                : connectors.holdSet(asset, target, key);       // place or modify

        return switch (r.code()) {
            case OK, ALREADY_APPLIED -> {
                projections.put(asset, new Projection(target, view.version()));
                yield Outcome.applied(asset, target);
            }
            case TEMPORARY  -> Outcome.retryLater(asset);         // backoff, same key
            case NOT_FOUND, PERMANENT -> Outcome.escalate(asset, r); // human task
            case INSUFFICIENT_FUNDS -> Outcome.retryOnNextBalanceEvent(asset);
        };
    }
}

Two properties matter more than the code. First, the idempotency key is the ledger version, not the order. Ten orders on one asset produce one hold, resized ten times, not ten holds. Second, the reconciler is convergent. Run it twice, run it after a crash, run it from two pods at once behind an advisory lock per asset: the end state is the same.

Drift is a signal, not something to fight

The ledger is authoritative, so the reconciler drives core back to target when it finds drift. But if the same asset drifts on every sweep, something outside the platform keeps changing it: a manual release, a hold expiry date in core, a batch job. Re-applying forever hides that. Cap automatic re-application per asset per day, then raise a human task with the before and after. Drift that repeats is a process problem, not a reconciliation problem.

Protected income share, computed centrally

Enforcement law commonly leaves part of a salary or benefit untouchable. Core banking systems often implement this natively. Prepaid cards, payroll cards and wallets that receive salary usually do not. The obligation is uniform; the substrate is not. The platform has to make the protection uniform anyway, which means the allowance is computed in one place and applied through the same target formula on every asset class.

Choose one of three tiers per asset, based on what its connector declares it can do.

TierWhenMechanism
DelegateConnector declares nativeProtectedSharePass the flag; core computes and enforces. The ledger allowance is zero for that asset.
Compute centrallyNo native protection, but partialHold is supportedThe platform computes the allowance and the reconciler holds the net amount, renewing every pay cycle.
GapNo partial hold, or no reliable income signalNever over-hold silently. Detect, escalate to a human carve-out, or mark the asset ineligible for amount holds while protection applies.

One guardrail binds all three: exactly one layer protects each asset. Native where it exists, central only where it does not. Two layers would carve the allowance out twice and under-enforce the order.

Central computation needs to know when income arrives. Two sources usually exist: a payroll file that states who was paid how much for which period, and a per-account credit notification that arrives faster but cannot tell salary from any other credit. Treat the file as the authority and the notification as a latency shortcut. Normalize both to one event and deduplicate on (party, asset, period). The allowance then renews exactly once per cycle, whatever order the two sources arrive in, and even if one never arrives.

Ship the gap tier before central computation

The compliance-critical failure is holding money the law protects. Under-enforcement is an audit finding; over-holding a protected salary is harm to a customer. Ship delegate and gap first: together they guarantee no silent over-hold. Central computation is the harder engineering and can follow once the reconciler has run in production for a few pay cycles.

Every order is a hash-chained stream

Each order is an event-sourced aggregate with its own stream. Two properties go beyond a standard event store.

Gap-free sequence per order. Each event carries a sequence number that starts at 1 and increments by exactly 1. An append succeeds only if the expected previous sequence matches, enforced by a unique constraint on (order_id, sequence). A missing number is detectable without any other source of truth.

Hash chain. Each event stores hash = H(prevHash || canonical(event)). Altering or deleting any event breaks every hash after it. Anchor the chain head periodically to write-once storage, and an auditor can verify an order’s full history with nothing but the stream and the anchor.

order-event.jsonjson
{
  "orderId": "7f1c2e9a-4b8d-4f0e-9a51-1d2c3b4a5e6f",
  "sequence": 6,
  "type": "ObligationReleased",
  "occurredAt": "2026-11-12T09:41:07.312Z",
  "definition": { "service": "lift", "version": "3.2.0" },
  "payload": {
    "releasesOrder": "order:block:a",
    "assetRef": "asset:opaque:01",
    "amount": { "value": "10000.00", "currency": "XXX" },
    "partial": true
  },
  "decision": { "rule": "lift.partial", "ruleVersion": "3.2.0", "inputsDigest": "sha256:9c1f…" },
  "prevHash": "sha256:4e07…",
  "hash": "sha256:b3a9…"
}

Note the decision block. Every event that reflects a choice records the rule, the rule version and a digest of the inputs. Replaying the same inputs against the same version gives the same outcome, which turns “why did the bank do this?” from an investigation into a lookup. Encrypt personal data inside payloads at field level and hash identity keys; the chain covers the ciphertext, so a key rotation does not break verification.

ChainVerifier.javajava
public Verification verify(List<StoredEvent> stream, Anchor anchor) {
    String prev = Hashes.GENESIS;
    long expectedSeq = 1;
    for (StoredEvent e : stream) {
        if (e.sequence() != expectedSeq++) return Verification.gap(e);
        if (!e.prevHash().equals(prev))   return Verification.broken(e);
        String h = Hashes.sha256(prev, Canonical.json(e.withoutHash()));
        if (!h.equals(e.hash()))          return Verification.tampered(e);
        prev = h;
    }
    return anchor.covers(prev) ? Verification.ok() : Verification.unanchored(prev);
}

Lifecycle and reversal from pre-terminal states

Orders follow an explicit state machine. Use the same state names in dashboards and alerts, so nobody has to reconstruct status from logs.

StateMeaningCan reverse?
RECEIVEDAccepted and acknowledgedYes
VALIDATINGSchema and rule checksYes
EXECUTINGObligations recorded; capability calls in flightYes
AWAITING_APPROVALMaker-checker required by policyYes
COMPOSING_OUTCOME / CALLBACK_PENDINGResult assembled; reply queued in an outboxYes
COMPLETED, REJECTED, DECLINEDTerminalNo — a later lift is a new order
REVERSING → REVERSAL_COMPLETEDCompensation per the service definition—

A reversal can arrive at any pre-terminal state. With stacked holds that is painful. With the ledger it is short: the reversal appends ObligationReleased for every active obligation of the order and moves to REVERSING; the reconciler recomputes the asset targets. If a capability call was mid-flight, its late reply is applied under an older ledger version and superseded on the next run. Compensation becomes data, not choreography.

A reversal that arrives before the order

Messages arrive out of order. Store a reversal for an unknown order as a tombstone keyed by the order reference, with a time-to-live. When the original order arrives, it is born reversed: recorded, audited and closed without ever creating an obligation. Rejecting the early reversal instead leaves an order that executes and is never undone.

Versioned service definitions, in-flight orders pinned

Block, garnish, lift, deny and ban should not be five code paths. They are five definitions executed by one engine. The engine knows a small number of choreography templates — request with callback, request with approval, synchronous inquiry, reversal compensation — and a definition picks a template and parameterizes it. A new service adds a definition; only a genuinely new shape needs a code release.

services/garnish.yamlyaml
service:
  id: garnish
  version: 2.4.0
  choreography: request-with-approval
  reversal: { supported: true, compensation: obligations.releaseAll }
  sla: { replyWithin: PT15M }

  validations:
    - rule: "request.amount == null || request.amount.value > 0"
      error: INVALID_AMOUNT

  obligations:                     # what the order writes to the ledger
    - when: "request.amount != null"
      type: AMOUNT_HOLD
      allocate: policy.allocation.default
    - when: "request.amount == null"
      type: FREEZE

  capabilities:                    # bound connectors must provide these
    - party.resolve
    - account.list
    - balance.inquire
    - hold.set

  approval: { required: byPolicy, roles: [maker, checker] }
  regression: scenarios/garnish/*.replay

Package definitions together with their schemas, outcome tables and regression scenarios into one immutable release unit. Sign it at build time, verify it on import, and promote it through environments the way you promote a container image. Most important: each order records the definition version it started on and stays pinned to it until it reaches a terminal state. A new version changes new orders only. That removes a whole class of incident — the rule that changed under an order already half-way through execution — and it makes every historical decision reproducible against the exact rules in force at the time.

Connector contracts, error taxonomy and certification

The engine never talks to a banking system directly. It calls capabilities, and a connector translates each capability for one system: core banking, the card platform, a wallet. Connectors are thin and stateless. All order state, and all audit, lives in the platform. Whether a connector runs as a flow on an integration server such as IBM App Connect Enterprise or as a small service on Red Hat OpenShift matters less than the contract it honours.

CapabilityShapeContract rule
party.resolveidentity → partyRefThe only place identity is translated
account.listpartyRef, filter → assetsFilter vocabulary owned by the platform
balance.inquireassetRef → available, ledgerMust expose balance excluding own hold
hold.set / hold.liftassetRef, amount, key → holdRefReplay-safe on the idempotency key
restriction.apply / .removeref, kind, key → restrictionRefDeny and ban class
payroll.inflowevents → normalized creditFile, notification, both or none

Connectors return capability results, never raw system status codes. Fix the error taxonomy in the contract: NOT_FOUND, INSUFFICIENT_FUNDS, ALREADY_APPLIED, TEMPORARY, PERMANENT. Five values, each with exactly one engine reaction. A connector that cannot map an error to one of them has not understood the system it wraps.

Each connector ships a manifest declaring its capabilities, versions and behaviour flags such as partialHold and nativeProtectedShare. Before a service is enabled, check that the bound connectors cover every capability its definition needs. A gap blocks activation; it never surfaces as a runtime failure on a live order.

Certification is a contract-test suite plus a simulator scenario pack, and it must run green before a connector is bound. The scenarios that catch real defects are not the happy paths:

  1. Duplicate delivery

    The same hold.set with the same key arrives twice, ten milliseconds apart, and again an hour later. The second and third calls must return ALREADY_APPLIED and leave exactly one hold.

  2. Reversal storms

    Hundreds of orders on overlapping assets are reversed at once, at every lifecycle state, including mid-call. End state: every asset at its recomputed target, no orphan holds, every stream verifiable.

  3. Out-of-order and superseded calls

    A call under ledger version 5 completes after version 6 was applied. The connector, or the reconciler on its next pass, must leave version 6 in force.

  4. Timeouts with unknown outcome

    The connector times out after core has applied the hold. The retry with the same key must converge, not double.

Failure modes

Two layers protecting the same income

A core system is upgraded and starts enforcing the protected share natively, but its connector manifest still says it does not. The platform computes an allowance on top of the native one and the order is under-enforced by the full allowance. Mitigation: version the manifest with the connector release, and include a certification scenario that fails if both layers protect the same asset.

Reconciler hammering a degraded core

A balance-event storm during a payroll run triggers a reconcile per credit. If core is already slow, the reconciler adds load at the worst moment. Mitigation: coalesce triggers per asset over a short window, keep a circuit breaker per connector, and let TEMPORARY back off. The target is a function of state, not of the trigger, so coalescing loses nothing.

A ledger rebuilt from a broken stream

If a projection rebuild silently skips an event, the ledger under-states obligations and the reconciler releases money it should hold. Mitigation: rebuilds verify every chain first and refuse to publish a ledger version derived from a stream with a gap or a broken hash.

Hold expiry configured in core

Some core hold primitives require an expiry date. If the connector sets one, the hold silently disappears while the obligation is still active, and the reconciler sees drift every time it expires. Mitigation: use the longest expiry the primitive allows, declare it in the manifest, and let the reconciler re-place before expiry rather than after.

Implementation checklist

  1. Agree the ownership split in writing

    The platform owns obligations; core owns money. Record it as an architecture decision and have legal, compliance and core banking owners sign it. Every later argument resolves against this line.

  2. Define obligation types before services

    Types, accumulation rules and how each projects to each asset class. Services are then definitions that emit typed obligations.

  3. Build the reconciler as a first-class component

    One function, four triggers, keyed on asset and ledger version, with drift reporting and a cap on automatic re-application. Test it with property-based tests: any sequence of obligation changes must converge to the same target.

  4. Make every order stream gap-free and chained

    Unique constraint on sequence, a hash per event, periodic anchors, and a verifier auditors can run themselves.

  5. Ship the protected-share gap tier first

    Delegate where core can, escalate where nothing can, and only then compute centrally. Never ship a path that can silently over-hold.

  6. Version service definitions and pin in-flight orders

    Sign, verify, promote, pin. Regression scenarios travel inside the release unit, so a definition cannot be promoted without its own tests.

  7. Certify connectors on the hard scenarios

    Duplicate delivery, reversal storms, superseded calls and unknown-outcome timeouts. A connector that passes only the happy path is not certified.
