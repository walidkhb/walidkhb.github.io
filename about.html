What shaped how I lead

Four chapters, each with a different vantage point: building software, engineering at national scale, consulting from the outside, and seven years inside one bank.

  1. 2012–2014Jordan, then Saudi Arabia

    Starting as an engineer

    I graduated in software engineering from Al-Balqa Applied University in Jordan in 2012 and started that July as a software engineer at SSSProcess, on enterprise process automation and solution delivery. In May 2014 I moved to Saudi Arabia as a senior software engineer at Arabian Centres.

    What I keptAn engineer’s test I still apply to every standard and control: does it make the right thing the easy thing?

  2. 2014–2017ELM, Riyadh

    Engineering at national scale

    At ELM, as a senior integration developer from December 2014 to September 2017, I worked on engineering for government-scale integration on national digital platforms, including Absher.

    What I keptA preference for platforms over projects. A platform leaves behind something the next team can build on.

  3. 2017–2018Zenith, Riyadh

    The view from outside

    From October 2017 to June 2018 I worked in consulting at Zenith, on enterprise integration and service architecture. It was a short chapter, and the first time I looked at integration from outside an organisation rather than from within one.

    What I keptMost organisations struggle not because of technology gaps, but because of structural fragmentation: unclear ownership, inconsistent standards and misaligned priorities.

  4. 2018–2025The Saudi Investment Bank, Riyadh

    Seven years from developer to vice president

    I joined The Saudi Investment Bank in July 2018 as a senior integration developer and led the zero-downtime migration of the legacy integration platform. As integration manager I designed the bank’s API monetisation strategy: partner platform, developer ecosystem and tiered pricing.

    In 2021 I founded the Digital Factory, reporting to C-level: a unit of 25 engineers, analysts and project managers that moved from waterfall to continuous delivery and shipped the bank’s first microservices application. I became acting head of integration in September 2023 and vice president in June 2025, seven years after joining.

    What I keptThe Digital Factory was the first time I built a unit rather than a system. Its DevSecOps practices became the standard for new development, and that is the outcome I now design for: capability the institution keeps.

Approach

What I believe, and where it shows

01

How I make difficult technology decisions

I start with ownership, not with the product: who owns the capability, who decides, and how a business priority becomes an architecture choice. The technology decision follows from that clarity, not the other way round.

Then I write the decision down and make it hold for everyone who builds on it, suppliers included.

Where it shows

  • Led the build-not-buy decision for Open Banking and the delivery of the bank’s own platform.

    Personally led

  • Authored the bank’s Integration Framework: ten standards, four review gates with threat modelling at design, and recorded architecture decisions. The same gates apply to vendors.

    Personally designed
02

Why organisational design matters to me

I focus on the organisational layer first. The most important technology work is not building systems; it is building the structures that let systems, teams and decisions scale.

Who owns each capability, who makes decisions, and where the line with suppliers sits are design choices. I make them as deliberately as architecture choices, because they decide whether technology execution is coherent.

Where it shows

  • Replaced direct access to the core with a catalogue of governed business APIs and a simulator, so projects test in parallel.

    Team-led delivery

  • Made delivery through the bank’s own source control, CI/CD and deployment pipeline a requirement in integration modernisation sourcing.

    Personally led
03

Innovation and risk in a regulated bank

In a bank, speed and control are not opposites when the control is designed in. I want standards, review gates and compliance checks that improve quality and reduce risk at the same time: automated and embedded, not manual gates that slow teams down. And I keep a person at the points where judgement matters.

Delivery

Where I move fastAbout two minutes from merge to a running build. GitOps is live on the development environment.

Where control holdsProduction deployment stays approval-gated. Promotion to higher environments is by change request.

AI

Where I move fastA personal Claude-native engineering practice, which keeps my view grounded in what the tools can really do.

Where control holdsBuilt the governed foundations AI needs (compliance gates on every change, secured APIs), contributed the integration view to the bank’s secure-by-design review for AI enablement, and called for a bank-wide policy on AI-assisted engineering.

04

What makes transformation last

Sustainable transformation requires ownership clarity, governance discipline and reusable capability. Everything else is project work with an expiration date.

So I judge a programme by what stays behind when it ends: capabilities with named owners, standards that teams and suppliers inherit, and platforms the next product can build on. When people leave, the capability should stay.

Where it shows

  • Launched two consumer digital banking products on a shared backbone connecting 15+ backend systems.

    Team-led delivery

  • Shortened time-to-market for new products and partners, with faster, repeatable partner onboarding.

    Team-led delivery
