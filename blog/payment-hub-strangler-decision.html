Strangle the Rails, Not the Channels: Migrating Payments One Scheme at a Time

When a bank buys a new payment hub, the first plan on the table is usually a cutover weekend. Every rail moves at once: instant payments, high-value RTGS, bill payments, bulk files, request-to-pay. Every channel is updated to call the new platform. The steering committee likes the clarity of a single date. Operations hates it, and operations is right.

A big-bang payment migration bundles every risk into one night. If the new platform misbehaves on one rail, all rails roll back. If one channel team is late, the date moves for everyone. And the rollback plan, when you read it closely, is usually “restore the old configuration and hope nothing was posted in between.”

There is a better shape, and it is not new. The strangler fig grows around an old tree until the tree can be removed. Applied to payments, it means moving one scheme at a time onto the hub, while the old path keeps running for everything else. The part that is easy to get wrong is where you put the seam.

Put the seam behind the channels, not in them

The most important decision is that the channel contract never changes. Mobile, internet banking, corporate portals and branch systems call one transfer API. They do not know, and must never need to know, whether a given payment is processed by the hub or by core banking. A router behind that API decides, per call, based on the scheme.

This sounds like an architectural nicety. It is actually a funding decision. If channels call the hub directly, every scheme migration becomes a change programme across every channel team, each with its own backlog, release train and test cycle. Moving one rail means coordinating six teams. If the seam is behind the channels, moving a rail is a configuration change in one place, owned by one team.

Older channels make this harder, and more important. Many banks still run channels that speak fixed-length records over message queues. They will not be rewritten for a payments programme, and they should not have to be. The answer is a second ingress adapter that converts those records into the same canonical payload and feeds the same router. Legacy channels reach the hub without a line of channel code changing. One router, two doors in.

If moving a payment rail requires a release from every channel team, you have not built a migration path. You have built a dependency graph, and the slowest team owns your timeline.

A flag per scheme, and what rollback really means

Each scheme gets its own routing flag. Turning it on sends new payments for that scheme to the hub. Turning it off sends them back to the old path. That is the rollback plan, and its simplicity is the point: no redeploy, no data migration, no channel change.

Leaders should understand the limit of that sentence. A flag rolls back new payments. Payments already in flight must finish on the engine they started on, because funds may already be reserved there and a scheme may already be waiting on a reply. The route is decided once, when the payment arrives, and stays with it. A flag that silently re-routes in-flight payments is not a rollback; it is a second incident.

The other limit is practice. A rollback that has never been exercised under load is a hope, not a control. Each flag should be turned off and on again in a rehearsal, with operations watching, before it is ever turned on in production.

Let evidence, not the calendar, choose the date

The question every steering committee asks is “when do we move SARIE?” The honest answer is: when the data says so. Dark launching is how you get that data.

In dark-launch mode, the router sends each payment for a scheme down the trusted path, as usual, and also down the new path as a shadow. The channel only ever sees the trusted result. The two outcomes are compared and every difference is logged: a different decision, a different amount, a different accounting entry, a different error code. When the discrepancy rate falls below an agreed threshold — in practice around 0.1% — the roles swap. The hub becomes the trusted path and the old path becomes the shadow. After a soak period with no material discrepancies, the old path for that scheme is switched off.

Three things make this work, and all three need someone senior to insist on them.

The shadow must have no side effects. A shadow call that places a real hold or books a real entry is not a test; it is a duplicate payment. The shadow path computes what it would do, and that is what gets compared. This needs to be enforced by the platform, not left to the discipline of each developer.

Not all discrepancies are equal. A rate of 0.1% means nothing until you know what is in it. A difference in the wording of an error message is noise. A difference in an accounting entry is a stop. The threshold has to be defined per category, and any discrepancy that touches money or the ledger is a blocker regardless of the overall rate.

The window has to include the hard days. A clean week in the middle of the month proves little. The comparison window should cover month-end, salary days and the busiest settlement cycles for that scheme. Those are the days the old path has years of quiet fixes for, and the new path has none.

Sequence by risk, and decide what never moves

Order matters. A sensible sequence starts with rails where the hub adds the most and the bank already has the least legacy: instant payments and request-to-pay are often new capabilities anyway, so there is less old behaviour to match. Bill payments and bulk files follow. High-value RTGS goes last, because the value per payment is highest and the tolerance for surprise is lowest.

Just as important is the list of things that never move. Transfers between a customer’s own accounts, internal transfers within the bank and similar flows have no scheme leg. They gain nothing from a payment hub. Writing that list down early stops the programme from drifting toward “everything on the new platform” as a goal in itself. The end state of a strangler migration is not an empty old system. It is an old system doing only what it is good at.

What leaders decide, and should not delegate

The engineering team can build the router, the flags, the shadow comparison and the dashboards. A handful of decisions need to be made above them, because they are about risk appetite and money, not code.

The cutover criteria per scheme. The discrepancy threshold, the categories that block regardless of rate, the minimum comparison window and the soak length. Agree them before the dark launch starts, not when the data comes in and everyone wants to go live.

Who may flip a flag, and when. Turning a scheme on in production is a change with customer impact. Turning it off in an incident must be fast. These are different authorities and both should be named, with a runbook that has been rehearsed.

The cost of running two paths. Dark launch is not free. Both engines run, both are monitored, discrepancies need people to triage them and reconciliation runs across both. Budget it explicitly, for every scheme, or the programme will be tempted to skip it for the later rails — which are the riskiest ones.

The date the old path dies. Strangler migrations fail in one predictable way: the old tree never comes down. Both paths stay live “just in case”, the bank pays for two, and every change has to be made twice. Each scheme’s plan needs a decommission step with an owner, triggered by the end of its soak period.

None of this is exotic. It is the discipline of changing a payment system while it carries live money: one rail at a time, behind a contract that does not move, with evidence deciding each step. The calendar will still matter. It just should not be the thing that decides.
