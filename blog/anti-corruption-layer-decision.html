The Anti-Corruption Layer Is a Boundary You Decide to Draw, Not an Engineering Convenience

When a team begins building new domain services that must integrate with a legacy core banking system, the technical decision in front of them is deceptively small: do we translate the CBS message format inside each service, or do we build a shared translation layer that all services consume from? The engineers will usually gravitate toward “inside each service” because it is faster and seems more self-contained. The VP of Enterprise Integration must override this. The shared translation layer — the anti-corruption layer — is not an architectural nicety. It is the boundary that determines whether the CBS can change without halting your roadmap.

Every team that builds a direct adapter to the CBS is writing a dependency on the CBS data model that it will defend for the next ten years. The ACL exists so that dependency lives in one place, owned by one team, and updated once.

The cost of not drawing the boundary

A KSA commercial bank typically has six to twelve domain service teams building against the CBS in parallel. Without an ACL, each team interprets the CBS message format independently. TxnAmt01 is the debit amount in one team’s code, the net settlement amount in another’s, and an undocumented field that the third team simply ignores. This is not a hypothetical. It is the reconciliation problem that surfaces 18 months into the modernisation programme, when the numbers from the payments team’s reporting service and the numbers from the accounts team’s balance service disagree by a small but non-zero amount that neither team can explain without looking at the other’s interpretation of the same CBS field.

When the CBS vendor then publishes a schema upgrade — version 4, adding two new fields and renaming three existing ones — the update that should be a one-team, one-week task becomes a twelve-team, twelve-week programme. Each team must find their CBS field references, understand what changed, test against a CBS environment, and deploy independently. The upgrade window for a schema change that should be invisible to the domain model becomes three months of coordination.

What the boundary actually costs

Building an ACL requires an upfront investment: typically two to four weeks of integration engineering to design the canonical model, implement the translation flows, write the contract tests, and set up the monitoring. It also requires ongoing ownership: the team that maintains the ACL must respond to CBS schema changes before domain teams are affected, which means they need advance notice from the CBS vendor (this is a governance requirement, not a technical one — someone has to put it in the contract).

The VP’s decision is straightforward when framed as a comparison:

  • Without ACL: each of twelve teams spends one week on the CBS schema upgrade (twelve weeks of engineering capacity) every time the CBS upgrades.
  • With ACL: one team spends one week on the CBS schema upgrade (one week of engineering capacity) and twelve teams see no change.

At a cadence of two CBS schema upgrades per year (conservative for a bank actively modernising), the ACL investment pays back in the first year. After that, it pays back continuously. The more teams that consume from the ACL, the larger the multiplier.

The team structure question

The ACL must be owned by a team, not by a committee. “Shared ownership” of an ACL is a maintenance vacuum: when the CBS schema changes on a Friday afternoon, no individual team feels responsible for updating the translation layer, and by Monday morning three domain service teams have failed health checks. Assign the ACL to the integration platform team (the team that owns IBM ACE, IBM MQ, and the Kafka clusters). They already own the infrastructure the ACL runs on; the translation logic is a natural extension of their responsibility.

The domain service teams are consumers of the canonical model, not maintainers of it. Their obligation is to review and approve changes to the canonical schema that affect them — that is a governance step, not an implementation task. Build this review into the change management process for the ACL: any change to the canonical schema requires sign-off from the consuming domain leads before it is deployed.

The ACL team owns the translation. The domain teams own the canonical model — it is their contract, and they must be able to reject a proposed change that breaks their domain semantics.

What the regulator cares about

SAMA’s operational resilience requirements (and the broader BCBS 239 risk data aggregation principles that apply to SIFIs) require that financial data flows are traceable end-to-end. The ACL is a natural enforcement point for that traceability: every CBS message that enters the ACL carries a CBS transaction reference; every canonical event that leaves the ACL carries that reference as an audit field. The chain is unbroken. Without an ACL, traceability depends on each of the twelve domain teams correctly carrying the CBS reference through their own systems — a governance requirement that is easy to overlook in a sprint and impossible to retrofit without data migration.

The ACL also provides a natural boundary for data quality monitoring. SAMA’s real-time gross settlement (RTGS) reporting requirements mean that payment data discrepancies discovered in reconciliation must be investigated and explained within defined timeframes. An ACL with a DLQ and translation-failure alerting makes those discrepancies visible immediately — a message that could not be translated never silently corrupts a downstream service’s state.

The decision

The VP of Enterprise Integration does not need to design the ACL — that is the platform team’s work. What the VP must decide, before the first integration sprint begins, is:

  • Who owns the ACL? Name the team and confirm it has capacity in the next sprint.
  • What is the canonical model governance process? Who proposes changes, who reviews them, who approves them, and what is the SLA for the approval?
  • What advance notice does the integration team need from the CBS vendor before a schema change? Build this into the vendor contract, not the incident process.
  • What is the DLQ escalation path? Who gets the alert at depth 10, and what is their expected response time?

These are governance decisions that cannot be made by an engineering team in a sprint planning meeting. They require VP sponsorship to hold up against the competing pressure of delivery timelines. The teams will build adapters if no one draws the boundary. That is the default. Drawing the boundary is the job.
