Circuit Breakers Are Not Optional: The Resilience Decision Every Integration Leader Defers Until Something Falls Over

There is a consistent pattern in how banks encounter cascade failure for the first time. It is always a quiet Tuesday afternoon. One backend — the SARIE payment gateway, the fraud screening engine, the CBS core banking adapter — starts responding slowly. Not erroring. Slowly. The monitoring system that watches for error rates does not alert, because the calls are technically succeeding. Meanwhile, the integration platform is accumulating hundreds of threads, each blocked waiting for a response that will eventually arrive in 15 seconds rather than the usual 200 milliseconds. The thread pool exhausts. Other services on the same platform — balance enquiries, account opening, card management — start timing out. The incident affects channels that had no dependency on the slow backend whatsoever. It takes the better part of an hour to diagnose, another hour to stabilise. SAMA gets an incident report. The board wants an explanation.

The explanation is always the same: there were no circuit breakers. The integration platform had no fault isolation between its downstream dependencies. When one dependency degraded, it consumed the resources shared by every other dependency. The bank had implicitly assumed that because each individual integration was reliable, the aggregate platform was also reliable. That assumption is false in any distributed system, and in a financial services integration estate with fifteen or more downstream dependencies, it is catastrophically false.

Cascade failure is not a rare event. It is the predictable outcome of a platform that has no mechanism to stop a slow upstream from consuming the resources used by an unrelated downstream.

Why this is a VP decision, not an engineering detail

Engineering teams know about circuit breakers. Most senior integration engineers have used Resilience4j or a similar library. The question is not whether the team knows how to implement them — it is whether the programme has time and mandate to implement them correctly across every downstream integration before the first incident. That is a resource and priority decision, and it sits with the VP.

Building fault isolation correctly takes more than adding a library dependency. Each downstream requires calibrated thresholds based on its actual failure profile. Each requires a tested fallback strategy — which for payment writes is a business decision, not a technical one: does the bank queue the payment and accept liability, or fail fast and return control to the customer? Each requires Prometheus alerts and a runbook for the operations team. Done properly across a fifteen-downstream integration estate, this is six to eight weeks of senior engineering effort. Done poorly — default thresholds, untested fallbacks, no metrics — it is worse than nothing, because it creates the illusion of protection while providing none.

The VP’s decision is whether to invest those six to eight weeks before the first incident or to wait and invest them reactively — typically in the two weeks following the cascade failure, under board scrutiny, with engineers who have not slept properly since the incident. The reactive investment costs significantly more and produces worse outcomes, because decisions made under pressure with incomplete sleep and a SAMA investigation open in the background are not the same decisions made in a planned programme sprint.

The specific decision on fallbacks

The hardest part of circuit breaker implementation in a banking context is not the code. It is the business decision about what to do when the breaker opens on a payment path. There are two defensible choices, and one that is not defensible.

Fail fast and return a 503. The payment is not submitted. The customer sees an error and can retry through another channel. The bank carries no liability because no payment was accepted. This is the appropriate response for synchronous payment initiation where the customer is present and alternative channels are available. It is clear, auditable, and carries no regulatory complexity.

Queue to a transactional outbox. The payment is accepted and queued locally. The bank returns a 202 Accepted and commits to executing when the gateway recovers. The bank has accepted a payment liability. This is appropriate for high-value corporate transfers and batch settlement flows where the customer expects the bank to absorb availability risk. It is more complex operationally: the queue must be monitored, the outbox must be processed when the gateway recovers, and the SLA for clearing the queue must be defined and contractually committed.

Silent queuing without a committed SLA is not defensible. If the bank tells the customer their payment was submitted, and that payment sits in a local queue for an indeterminate period while the gateway is unreachable, that is a SAMA compliance question at minimum and a liability question if the payment was time-sensitive. The fallback must be a documented business commitment, not an engineering implementation detail.

The VP must make this call, documented and signed off, for every payment path in the integration estate. It is not something that can be delegated to the engineering lead to decide at implementation time.

What the regulator cares about

SAMA’s Technology Risk Management Framework requires banks to demonstrate that their systems can sustain the failure of individual components without cascading to broader platform outages. This requirement is typically assessed through resilience testing — a simulated downstream failure injected into the production-like environment — and through incident analysis when real failures occur. A bank that experiences a cascade failure and cannot show that circuit breakers were in place (or that their absence was a known risk with a remediation timeline) has a difficult conversation with its technology risk regulator.

The circuit breaker implementation also feeds directly into the bank’s Business Continuity documentation. For a payment-critical downstream like SARIE, the BCP must describe the degraded operating mode when that downstream is unavailable for an extended period. If the BCP says “manual processing via alternative channel,” the circuit breaker must automatically route to that channel — not require a human to update a configuration file during an incident. The fallback is the BCP; the BCP must be implemented, not just documented.

What the team needs from leadership to ship it

The engineering team implementing fault isolation needs two things from the VP that cannot be delivered at the technical level. The first is protected sprint capacity. Circuit breaker implementation is infrastructure work — it does not deliver a user-visible feature, and it will be deprioritised in favour of feature delivery every time there is sprint pressure, unless the VP explicitly holds the capacity. The six to eight weeks required must be programmed into the delivery plan, not treated as a nice-to-have that fits in the gaps.

The second is a decision on the fallback strategy for each payment path, documented and available before the team implements the fallback code. The engineering team should not be making business liability decisions at implementation time. If the team cannot get a documented fallback decision from the business within two weeks of asking, the VP needs to escalate. An undecided fallback means the implementation either ships with a default that may be wrong, or waits for a business decision and misses the delivery window.
