Reconciliation Pipelines: The Data Integrity Decision That Regulators Actually Examine

Most banks that have modernised their data integration stack — replacing batch file transfers with CDC pipelines and event-driven architectures — have invested heavily in the plumbing: Debezium connectors, Kafka topics, Flink jobs, schema registries. What they have underinvested in, almost universally, is the proof that the plumbing worked. CDC tells you what the database did. Reconciliation tells you whether the downstream received and correctly applied it. Only one of those is a data integrity control that a regulator will accept as evidence.

SAMA’s Technology Risk Management framework is explicit: data integrity controls must be demonstrable across system boundaries. “Our CDC pipeline is running” is not that demonstration. A reconciliation process with a stored result log, a break investigation trail, and a tamper-evident archive is.

What the regulator will look for

In a SAMA TRM examination focused on data integrity, the examination team will ask for three categories of evidence. First: is there an automated, scheduled process that compares source and downstream for every material data flow? “We check it manually when there are complaints” is a failed control. Second: when a discrepancy is detected, how is it classified, investigated, and resolved? The expectation is a documented process with timestamped evidence, not a Slack message. Third: can you prove that the reconciliation records themselves have not been modified after the fact? An audit log that can be amended is not an audit log.

The examination team is not looking for perfect systems — they know CDC pipelines produce occasional breaks. They are looking for evidence of a functioning control: that breaks are detected quickly, that they are investigated systematically, and that they are resolved within a defined SLA. A bank that can show a clean reconciliation history with zero unresolved breaks and a documented investigation log for every historical break is in a stronger examination position than a bank that claims its data pipeline never fails.

The question is not whether your system produces data integrity errors. The question is how quickly you detect them and what you do about it. That is the control the regulator is testing.

The decision the VP has to make

Reconciliation pipelines are not expensive to build once the team understands the pattern. They are expensive to build correctly the first time if the scope is not defined at the leadership level before the engineering work begins. The four decisions that require VP-level input are:

Which data flows are in scope? Not every internal data sync needs a formal reconciliation pipeline — a dev environment cache does not. But any data flow that touches payment amounts, account balances, customer identity data, or regulatory reporting inputs is material. The compliance team needs to define the boundary; the engineering team cannot define it for them, and the outcome of getting it wrong is a control gap that appears in the next examination.

What is the SLA for break resolution? SAMA does not prescribe a number, but the bank’s own risk appetite framework does. A four-hour SLA for detecting and escalating an unresolved break is a reasonable production commitment for a bank at SAIB’s scale. A 24-hour SLA is defensible if it is documented and consistently met. An undefined SLA is a control weakness that the examination team will note as such.

What is the retention period? Regulatory records retention in Saudi Arabia is governed by SAMA and, for listed institutions, CMA. The bank’s internal policy derived from those requirements is the authority. For data integrity records, seven years is the conservative position. The storage cost is trivial; the governance requirement to define and enforce an immutable retention policy is not trivial, and it requires sign-off from Legal and Records Management, not just the engineering team.

Who owns the control? A reconciliation pipeline that runs automatically is still a control owned by a human. Automated breaks that are not picked up by an on-call engineer within the SLA are SLA breaches that accumulate in the investigation log and become evidence of a poorly-operated control. The VP needs to designate ownership — Operations, the integration platform team, or a dedicated data integrity function — and ensure that on-call rotas and escalation paths are documented before the pipeline goes live in a regulated environment.

What leadership can do to accelerate the build

The engineering work to build a reconciliation pipeline is well-understood: fingerprinting, hash comparison, a break store schema, classification logic, alerting wiring. The work that blocks it is almost never technical — it is alignment. Three organizational blockers appear on every reconciliation project at a bank.

The first is the definition of the column set to hash. Which fields must be identical between source and downstream, and which are expected to differ (technical timestamps, version numbers, downstream-specific enrichments)? This seems like a database question. In practice it requires agreement between the source system owner, the downstream system owner, and Risk — three separate reporting lines, often three separate project budgets. A VP who can convene that conversation and force a decision in a single meeting saves weeks.

The second is access to the downstream for fingerprint extraction. Reconciliation requires reading from the downstream system at query time, in a read-only, non-customer-impacting way. For a third-party system or a shared platform service, that access requires a formal request, a security review, and often a contract amendment. Starting that access request the day the reconciliation project is approved, not the day the engineering team asks for it, typically saves four to six weeks of elapsed time.

The third is the decision about what to do when the pipeline detects a break that cannot be auto-resolved. An automated replay that re-applies source data to the downstream is a powerful recovery mechanism — but it requires governance approval before the first production run, because a misconfigured replay can overwrite correct downstream data with stale source data and create a larger break than the one it was trying to fix. Leadership needs to define the approval gate: who can authorize a manual replay, what evidence is required before triggering it, and what the rollback procedure is if it makes things worse.

A reconciliation pipeline built with those decisions made in advance is a genuine data integrity control. One built without them is a monitoring dashboard that tells you there are breaks but gives the operations team no authority to act on them.
