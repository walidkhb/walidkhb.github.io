SAMA Open Banking Phase 2: What Implementation Taught Us About What Comes Next

Phase 1 of Saudi Arabia's Open Banking framework — Account Information Services — is no longer a future state. The APIs are in production at every Saudi bank that operates retail current and savings accounts. Account aggregation use cases are live with licensed third-party providers. The framework that SAMA published in 2022 has, in operational terms, become routine. The implementation phase is over. The next phase is not.

Phase 2 of the framework — Payment Initiation Services and the broader extension into premium API products — is now the active strategic question. The technical specifications are published. The regulatory expectations are clear. What is less clear, judging from the conversations happening across the sector, is whether banks have understood that Phase 2 is not a continuation of Phase 1. It is a different operating model, with different revenue logic, different risk surface, and different organizational demands. Treating it as more of the same is the most expensive mistake an institution can make.

Phase 1 was a compliance programme that produced a technology asset. Phase 2 is a product programme that requires a compliance discipline. The institutions that recognise the difference will lead the next eighteen months. The institutions that don't will spend that period rebuilding what they should have built once.

The lessons from Phase 1 implementation — the ones that actually matter for Phase 2 — are not technical. They are organizational, commercial, and architectural. They are the lessons that emerge after the APIs are live and the institution has to live with the operating model it inadvertently built.

What Phase 1 actually built

The APIs that Phase 1 produced are real, and they work. But the way they were built reflects the way the programme was scoped: as a regulatory implementation. The teams that delivered Phase 1 were typically integration and security teams under compliance pressure, with a fixed deadline and a published specification. That framing produced APIs that meet the specification and clear the regulator's bar. It also produced APIs that, in many institutions, do not have a product owner, do not have a roadmap, do not have commercial accountability, and do not have a clear path to evolve beyond the regulated minimum.

The operational reality of Phase 1 in many Saudi banks is that the open banking APIs sit organizationally adjacent to the rest of the bank's API estate, but with a different governance model and a different ownership structure. They are run as a regulatory obligation. The integration patterns, the consent management, the dispute handling, the onboarding flows for third-party providers — all of these were built specifically for Phase 1 and are not architecturally continuous with the bank's broader API platform. The result is an open banking estate that is technically compliant and commercially inert.

This is the inheritance that Phase 2 will be built on top of. And it is the part of Phase 1 that most banks will need to revisit before they can credibly run Phase 2.

Why Phase 2 changes the operating model

Payment Initiation Services are not an extension of Account Information Services with a few new endpoints. They are a fundamentally different product. Account Information is read-only, broadly low-risk, and produces value in aggregation. Payment Initiation is write-path, high-risk, and produces value in the payment flow itself. The integration with the bank's core, the controls around authorisation and authentication, the fraud surface, the dispute resolution mechanics, and the customer experience expectations are all qualitatively different.

The institutions that succeed in Phase 2 will be the ones that recognise it as a payment product first and a regulatory implementation second. That recognition has organizational consequences. Payment Initiation requires product ownership at a level that Phase 1 did not. It requires customer experience design that competes with the bank's own mobile channel. It requires fraud and dispute capabilities that operate in real time, not in retrospect. It requires commercial accountability for the success of third-party integrations — because if the third-party experience is bad, the customer's perception of the bank suffers, even though the bank is technically the upstream service provider.

None of this is captured in the technical specifications. All of it is what will determine whether Phase 2 produces commercial value or compliance cost.

The premium API conversation that should be happening now

SAMA's framework explicitly anticipates premium APIs — capabilities that go beyond the regulated minimum and that banks can offer to ecosystem partners on commercial terms. This is the strategic opportunity that Phase 1 most banks missed and that Phase 2 makes urgent.

The regulated APIs are, by definition, undifferentiated. Every licensed bank in the Kingdom is required to expose the same Account Information and Payment Initiation capabilities. There is no competitive advantage in having them, and there is significant competitive disadvantage in not. The premium APIs are different. They are the bank's choice. They reflect the institution's product capability, its commercial appetite, and its assessment of where ecosystem demand will create value. They are also the only place in the open banking framework where a bank can compete on more than execution quality.

The institutions that will define the next chapter of Saudi banking digital infrastructure are the ones that are now building premium API products in the categories that the market actually wants — transaction enrichment, merchant settlement intelligence, FX optimization, embedded finance primitives, real-time risk scoring — and exposing them on commercial terms to fintech partners, large merchants, and other banks. These are not regulated products. They are commercial products. They require pricing, packaging, partner management, SLA commitments, and a different operating model than the regulated APIs require.

Phase 2 is the moment when this conversation has to happen at the executive level. The premium API strategy needs to be defined before Phase 2 development crowds out the architectural space and the political bandwidth to do it. The institutions that wait until Phase 2 is in production to consider premium APIs will find that the operating model they built for Phase 2 is not capable of running them, and they will be rebuilding rather than extending.

The architecture decisions that will not survive Phase 2

Several Phase 1 architectural choices that worked operationally for Account Information will not survive Phase 2. The institutions that have already started reviewing them are the ones positioned to lead. The institutions that intend to bolt Phase 2 onto Phase 1 infrastructure will find themselves rebuilding under operational pressure.

Consent and authorisation flows. The consent model that suffices for read-only access to account information is not adequate for write-path payment initiation. The authorisation strength, the customer authentication ceremony, the consent revocation guarantees, and the audit trail expectations all change. Banks that built consent infrastructure as a Phase 1 deliverable rather than as a reusable platform component will need to rebuild it.

Real-time integration with the core. Account Information can tolerate eventual consistency — a balance that is two seconds stale is acceptable for most use cases. Payment Initiation cannot. The integration between the open banking layer and the bank's payment infrastructure must be real-time, idempotent, and operationally resilient at the level of regulated payment systems. Banks that built Phase 1 on top of cached data feeds will discover that the same approach does not work for Phase 2.

Fraud and risk surface. The fraud profile of Phase 2 is materially different. A third-party-initiated payment is a vector that the bank's existing fraud systems were not designed for. The signals that fraud models rely on — device fingerprints, channel patterns, behavioral baselines — arrive in different shapes in third-party flows. Banks that have not started building third-party-aware fraud capability are going into Phase 2 with a meaningfully exposed risk surface.

Operational continuity. Phase 1 incidents are recoverable. A degraded Account Information API is an inconvenience. A degraded Payment Initiation API at peak time is a payment system incident with regulatory and reputational consequences. The operational model — the on-call coverage, the incident response, the change management discipline — needs to be at the level the bank applies to its core payment infrastructure, not at the level it applied to Phase 1 read-only services.

What the executive conversation needs to cover

The right Phase 2 conversation at the executive level is not a programme update. It is a strategic decision about how the institution intends to compete in the open banking era and what operating model it needs to build to do that responsibly.

That conversation needs to address questions that are typically deferred. Who owns the open banking estate as a product portfolio — not as a regulatory deliverable? What is the commercial model for premium APIs, and which products will the institution lead with? How will the bank measure success in Phase 2 — by adoption, by transaction value, by partner ecosystem growth, by share of the third-party-initiated payment volume in the Kingdom? What investment is the bank prepared to make in the operating model required to run a payment-grade API platform, distinct from the integration team that delivered Phase 1?

These are leadership questions. The technology choices that follow from clear answers are tractable. The technology problems that result from not having the conversation are the ones that will define the difficulty of the next eighteen months.

The window that is closing

The institutions that will lead Saudi Open Banking in the years ahead are the ones that are using the period between Phase 1 stabilisation and Phase 2 production to make the architectural and organizational decisions that Phase 2 will not give them time to make later. The window for those decisions is now — before development pressure, deadline pressure, and integration commitments lock the operating model in place.

Phase 1 was the compliance programme. Phase 2 is the product programme. The institutions that recognise the difference will compete in the open banking era. The ones that don't will pay the cost of the assumption that delivering the specification is the same as delivering the capability. SAMA's framework has given the Kingdom an open banking infrastructure that, on a global comparison, is clean, well-specified, and ambitious. What it has not done, and cannot do, is decide for any individual institution whether to treat that infrastructure as a regulatory cost or a strategic opportunity. That decision is the one that defines what Phase 2 will mean for each bank that has to make it.
