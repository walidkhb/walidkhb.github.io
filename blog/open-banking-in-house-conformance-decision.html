Regulatory Interfaces Are Platform Capability: Why We Built Our Open Banking Authorisation Server In-House

Most banks treat Open Banking as a compliance purchase. The regulator publishes a standard, a product promises conformance, and the programme becomes a procurement and integration exercise. That route works. It is also the route where the bank ends up owning the obligation but not the capability.

We took the other route. A small team built the bank side of the KSA Open Banking Standard in-house: the FAPI 2.0 authorisation server, the account information APIs and the payment initiation APIs. In sandbox, it passed the regulator’s functional conformance suites 130 of 130 — 67 of 67 for account information, and 32 of 32 and 31 of 31 on the two payment initiation suites. A strict dry run against the OpenID Foundation FAPI 2.0 suite finished with zero failures. The certification programme is under way. These are sandbox results, and I will keep calling them that.

This essay is not about the code. It is about why the decision was worth making, and what it asks of leadership.

Why build what you could buy

The honest argument for buying is speed. The honest argument against is that Open Banking is not a one-off deliverable. The standard has errata. Profile versions disagree on details such as how long a signed request may live. New API families arrive: payments today, other products tomorrow. Each change lands on whoever holds the code.

If that is a vendor, every change is a change request, a release cycle and a negotiation. If it is your own team, it is a configuration change or a sprint. Over a five-year horizon, the second model is not only cheaper. It is the only one where the bank can answer the regulator’s next question in weeks rather than quarters.

There was a second reason. A FAPI 2.0 authorisation server is not exotic. It is OAuth done carefully: signed requests, certificate-bound tokens, consent tied to every call. Those are the same controls the bank needs for corporate APIs, partner integrations and internal high-value services. Building it once, properly, gives the institution a security pattern it can reuse well beyond Open Banking.

A regulatory interface you buy is a cost. A regulatory interface you build, and can prove, is a capability the rest of the platform can stand on.

What it gives the institution

Evidence, not assurance. Every rule in the security profile maps to one place in code and to the conformance tests that hit it. When an auditor asks how the bank enforces short-lived, certificate-bound tokens, the answer is a table, a line of code and a test verdict. Not a product datasheet.

Conformance on every change. We scripted the regulator’s conformance runner to run without a person at the keyboard. A full pass now takes under an hour. Conformance stopped being an annual event and became a build gate. That is the difference between being compliant on the day of the test and staying compliant between tests.

A team that understands the standard. The engineers who built it can read a new errata and say what changes. That knowledge is hard to hire and impossible to buy by the hour. It is also what makes the bank a credible voice when the regulator consults the market.

Freedom on the back end. The Open Banking layer talks to the bank’s systems through one narrow adapter. The sandbox uses a simulator behind it. Production can connect to whatever the bank runs, through its existing integration layer on IBM and Red Hat platforms, without the regulatory surface caring.

The decisions leaders own

Building in-house does not remove decisions. It moves them to where they belong. Four of them cannot be delegated to the engineering team.

Which profile version the bank commits to. The KSA standard and the OpenID Foundation’s KSA profile do not agree on every number. One allows a signed request to be an hour old; the other allows ten minutes. Engineering made it configurable. Leadership must decide which one the bank certifies against and own that position with the regulator.

How strict the bank is when test tools disagree with the text. Conformance tools are software, and software has quirks. We handled each one with a named, logged switch: relaxed for the functional suite, strict for everything else. The strict OpenID Foundation run is the proof that the switches are compatibility, not weakness. But the policy — strict by default, relaxed only in a named test profile, never in production — is a risk decision. It needs a signature above the team.

Where the trust boundary sits. We shipped the authorisation server and the resource APIs as one deployable, on purpose. They share the consent record in-process, so there is no internal service-to-service trust to secure. That is simpler and safer today. It also means scaling them independently later is a deliberate redesign, not a configuration change. Leaders should know they made that trade.

How results are described. “Passed conformance in sandbox” and “certified” are different statements with different consequences. The temptation to round up is strong when a board slide needs a headline. Resist it. The precise sentence is the one that survives the regulator reading it back to you.

What I would tell a peer starting now

Start with the conformance suite, not the architecture diagram. The suite is the regulator’s definition of done, and most of it tests what your server refuses, not what it allows. Automate it in the first month. Keep a single table that maps every rule to one enforcement point. And give a small, senior team the mandate to own the standard end to end.

The result is not just a passing score. It is an institution that can treat each new regulatory interface as a feature of its own platform, delivered on its own schedule.
