Strangle the Rails, Not the Channels: How to Migrate Payment Schemes Without a Big-Bang Cutover

Sooner or later, most banks move their payment schemes onto a new processing platform. The trigger varies — a new instant payment rail, the move to ISO 20022, an ageing switch that no longer scales — but the first plan on the table is nearly always the same: a cutover weekend. Every rail moves at once. Every channel is changed to call the new platform. The steering committee likes the clarity of a single date. Operations dislikes it, and operations is right.

A big-bang payment migration concentrates every risk into one night. If the new platform misbehaves on one scheme, every scheme rolls back. If one channel team is late, the date moves for all of them. And the rollback plan, read closely, usually amounts to restoring the old configuration and hoping nothing was posted in between.

The alternative is old and well understood. The strangler fig grows around a tree until the tree can be removed. In payments, that means moving one scheme at a time onto the new platform while the existing path keeps carrying everything else. The pattern is easy to name. The value comes from a handful of decisions about where the seam goes, how evidence is gathered, and who is allowed to say “go”.

Put the seam behind the channels, not in them

The decision that matters most is that the channel contract does not change. Mobile, internet banking, corporate portals and branch systems call one payment API. They do not know, and should never need to know, which engine processes a given payment. A router behind that API decides per call, based on the scheme.

This looks like an architectural preference. It is really a funding and scheduling decision. If channels call the new platform directly, every scheme migration becomes a coordinated release across every channel team, each with its own backlog and test cycle. Moving one rail means aligning half a dozen teams. With the seam behind the channels, moving a rail is a configuration change in one place, owned by one team.

Older channels make this more important, not less. Many institutions still run channels that exchange fixed-length records over message queues. They will not be rewritten for a payments programme and should not have to be. A second ingress adapter that converts those records into the same canonical request lets them reach the new platform through the same router, without a line of channel code changing.

If moving one payment scheme needs a release from every channel team, you do not have a migration path. You have a dependency graph, and the slowest team owns your timeline.

One flag per scheme

Each scheme gets its own routing flag, with a small number of positions: existing path only; existing path trusted with the new platform as a silent shadow; new platform trusted with the existing path as the shadow; new platform only. Moving between positions is a configuration change, not a deployment.

The granularity is the point. A single global switch recreates the big bang in miniature. A flag per scheme lets instant payments move while high-value transfers stay where they are, and lets a problem on bill payments be contained to bill payments. Where a scheme is large, the flag can be narrowed further — by channel, by customer segment, by amount band — but the scheme is the natural unit, because that is where the operational and settlement boundaries already sit.

Dark launch, with thresholds agreed in advance

The question every steering committee asks is “when do we move this scheme?” The honest answer is: when the evidence says so. Dark launching is how the evidence is produced.

In dark-launch mode, each payment goes down the trusted path as normal and is also evaluated by the new path as a shadow. The customer only ever sees the trusted result. The two outcomes are compared field by field — the decision, the amount, the accounting entries, the error code — and every difference is logged and categorised.

Three conditions make the comparison meaningful, and each needs someone senior to insist on it.

The shadow must have no side effects. A shadow call that reserves funds or books an entry is not a test; it is a duplicate payment. The shadow computes what it would do, and that is what gets compared. This has to be enforced by the platform, not left to the care of each developer.

Discrepancies are not all equal. A headline rate — say, below 0.1% — means nothing until you know what is in it. A difference in the wording of a rejection message is noise. A difference in an accounting entry is a stop. Thresholds must be set per category, and any discrepancy that touches money or the ledger blocks cutover regardless of the overall rate.

The window must include the hard days. A clean fortnight in mid-month proves little. The comparison window should cover month-end, salary days, public-holiday backlogs and the heaviest settlement cycles for that scheme. Those are the days the existing path has years of quiet fixes for, and the new one has none.

Cutover as a decision, not a date

When the thresholds are met over the agreed window, the roles swap: the new platform becomes the trusted path and the existing one becomes the shadow. That swap is a decision record, not a calendar entry. It should state the window covered, the discrepancy rate by category, the open items and who accepted them, and the reconciliation results over the same period.

After the swap comes a soak period, with the old path still shadowing. Only when the soak ends cleanly is the existing path for that scheme switched off. Each step has an entry criterion and an owner. A programme can still publish target dates — it should — but the dates are forecasts of when the evidence is expected to be ready, not commitments that override it.

What rollback actually means

“Turn the flag off” is a good rollback plan, and its simplicity is a feature: no redeployment, no data migration, no channel change. Leaders should understand three limits on it.

First, a flag rolls back new payments only. Payments already in flight must finish on the engine they started on, because funds may already be reserved there and the scheme may already be waiting on a reply. The route is decided once, when the payment arrives, and stays with it. A flag that re-routes in-flight payments is not a rollback; it is a second incident.

Second, rollback is only cheap while the old path is still warm. Once it has been decommissioned, “rollback” means an emergency re-implementation. That is why the soak period exists, and why its length is a risk decision rather than an engineering one.

Third, a rollback that has never been exercised under load is a hope, not a control. Every flag should be switched back in a rehearsal, with operations watching, before it is ever relied upon in production.

Sequence by risk, and decide what never moves

Order matters. A sensible sequence starts where the new platform adds the most and the existing path carries the least history: newer rails such as instant payments and request-to-pay often have less legacy behaviour to match. Bill payments and bulk files tend to follow. High-value RTGS usually goes last, because the value per payment is highest and the tolerance for surprise is lowest.

Equally important is the list of things that never move. Transfers between a customer’s own accounts and other internal transfers have no scheme leg; they gain little from a payment platform. Writing that list down early stops the programme drifting toward “everything on the new platform” as a goal in itself. The end state of a strangler migration is not an empty old system. It is an old system doing only what it is good at, or nothing at all — by design, not by default.

What leaders decide, and should not delegate

Engineering can build the router, the flags, the shadow comparison and the dashboards. Five decisions sit above them, because they are about risk appetite, accountability and money rather than code.

The cutover criteria per scheme. Discrepancy categories, thresholds, the categories that block regardless of rate, the minimum comparison window and the soak length. Agree them before the dark launch starts, not when the data arrives and everyone is eager to go live.

Who may move a flag, and when. Moving a scheme forward in production is a change with customer impact and needs change authority. Moving it back during an incident must be fast and should sit with the on-call operations lead. These are different authorities; both should be named, and both runbooks rehearsed.

The cost of running two paths. Dark launch is not free. Both engines run, both are monitored, discrepancies need people to triage them, and reconciliation spans both. Budget it explicitly for every scheme, or the programme will be tempted to skip it for the later rails — which are the riskiest ones.

Customer-facing consistency. Rejection reasons, notification timing and statement narratives should not change because the engine did. Somebody has to own that list and sign off that the new path matches it, or explain each deliberate difference.

The date the old path is retired. Strangler migrations fail in one predictable way: the old tree never comes down. Both paths stay live “just in case”, the institution pays for two, and every regulatory or scheme change has to be built twice. Each scheme’s plan needs a decommission step with a named owner, triggered by the end of its soak.

None of this is exotic. It is the discipline of changing a payment system while it carries live money: one scheme at a time, behind a contract that does not move, with evidence deciding each step. The calendar still matters. It just should not be the thing that decides.
