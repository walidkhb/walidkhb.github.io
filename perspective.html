I. The Future

The Future of Banking Technology in Saudi Arabia

The next decade of Saudi banking will be defined less by technology choices and more by the institutional capability to absorb change at pace. Cloud, AI, real-time payments, and ecosystem banking are no longer the question. The question is whether a bank is organized to keep adopting them year after year, under SAMA expectations, without exhausting itself in the process.

Vision 2030 has shifted the centre of gravity. Saudi banks are no longer competing only on product or distribution. They are competing on operating model — how quickly the institution can take a regulatory mandate, a partner opportunity, or a customer expectation and convert it into a deployed capability with controls intact.

In my view, the institutions that will lead are the ones that treat technology as a long-term capability investment rather than a sequence of programs. That means platforms that compound, governance that accelerates, and leadership benches deep enough to absorb scale without losing coherence.

The headline technology will keep changing. The discipline of building an institution that can keep absorbing it is the durable advantage.

II. Regulation

Open Banking Beyond Compliance

SAMA’s open banking framework is the floor, not the ceiling. Treated as a checkbox, it produces a small set of regulated APIs and a closed compliance posture. Treated as a strategic platform, it becomes the foundation for ecosystem banking — the same surface that satisfies the regulator also enables fintech partnerships, Banking-as-a-Service, and embedded finance.

The decision is upstream of technology. It is an executive choice about institutional posture. Will the bank participate in the ecosystem as a regulated exporter of services, or remain a consumer of distribution channels owned by others?

The institutions that own the answer early build the partner governance, consent management, and operating discipline that turn open banking into commercial optionality. The institutions that defer the question end up rebuilding the same surface twice.

III. Growth

Why Integration Is a Growth Engine

Integration is often described as plumbing. That framing is wrong, and it is expensive. Integration determines how quickly a bank can launch a new product, onboard a new partner, comply with a new regulation, or absorb an acquisition. It is the operating leverage of the entire institution.

When integration is treated as a strategic capability — owned, funded, governed, and measured — the bank’s marginal cost to launch falls with each program. New digital products land in weeks. Partners onboard in days. Regulatory mandates land predictably instead of disruptively.

When integration is treated as a cost centre, the opposite happens. Every program rebuilds the same surface, every partner gets a custom connection, and every regulatory cycle becomes an emergency. The board sees the symptom — slow delivery — without seeing the cause.

The work of an executive technology leader is to make this visible and reposition integration as what it actually is: the institution’s growth engine.

IV. Doctrine

Legacy Modernization Without Disruption

Most banks defer platform modernization because the perceived risk feels higher than the cost of standing still. That calculation is almost always wrong. The cost of standing still compounds quietly — in delivery speed, in cost-to-serve, in regulatory friction, in talent retention. The risk of modernization is visible. The risk of inertia is not.

A disciplined doctrine makes modernization safer than inertia:

Strangle, do not replace. Move capability to the new foundation incrementally. Keep the legacy operational until the new platform proves itself in production.

Govern continuity as a first-class commitment. Every program is delivered with explicit service-continuity SLOs, not as an afterthought. Zero customer-facing downtime is an outcome that gets designed for — not hoped for.

Modernize for growth, not for technology. Each modernization program funds a new capability the institution can sell, satisfy a regulator with, or scale faster on. Technology change without a business outcome is theatre.

The doctrine works. I have applied it to mission-critical bank platforms and shipped without a single second of service interruption. Modernization is a managed risk, not a hazardous one — provided it is led with discipline.

V. People

Building High-Performance Technology Organizations

The most consequential work an executive technology leader does is not platform or program. It is people. The institution’s technology capability is the capability of the people running it — and that capability is built deliberately or not at all.

High-performance organizations have four traits in common. Clear ownership, so accountability is real. Honest operating cadence, so issues surface early. Real investment in leaders, so the bench gets deeper each year. And a culture that protects standards while removing friction — making the right thing the easy thing.

I measure my own leadership by the depth of the bench I leave behind — not the number of programs I closed. Programs end. Institutions are built by people who stay, grow, and lead the next decade. That investment is the most strategic technology decision an executive can make.
